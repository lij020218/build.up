## PATTERN 1: Radical Focus -- Kill Everything Except the Core {#pattern-1}

### The Principle
**"When you are dying, the cure is not to do more -- it is to do less, but do it perfectly."**

### Evidence Across Cases

**Apple (1997):** When Steve Jobs returned, Apple was 90 days from bankruptcy. His first move was not to innovate -- it was to destroy. He slashed the product line from dozens of models to exactly four (desktop/portable x consumer/professional). He killed the Newton, ended the clone program, cut 70% of inventory. Only after this radical subtraction did he launch the "Think Different" campaign and begin the iMac project. Result: Apple went from -$1B/year to the most valuable company in history.

**LEGO (2003):** Losing $1 million per day with $800 million in debt. New CEO Knudstorp asked: "What if the problem is LEGO itself?" He cut SKUs from 13,000 to 7,000, sold the theme parks, shut down the video game division, and killed the clothing line. He returned LEGO to its core: the brick. Operating margin recovered from 2.4% to 15.6% within two years.

**Samsung (1993-1998):** Lee Kun-hee's famous Frankfurt Declaration -- "Change everything except your wife and children" -- was actually about radical subtraction. He sold 10 subsidiaries, cut the group from 65 affiliates to 45, and laid off 50,000 people to focus on three industries: electronics, engineering, and chemicals. When the 1997 IMF crisis hit, Samsung survived because it had already stripped away the fat.

**Starbucks (2008):** Howard Schultz returned as CEO and immediately closed 900 stores and laid off 6,700 employees. He shut every remaining store for 3+ hours to retrain baristas. He eliminated bureaucracy and refocused on one thing: the emotional connection between barista and customer. Profits rebounded from $315M to $945M within two years.

### The Korean SMB Application
**The typical Korean small business owner tries to do everything** -- run social media, manage inventory, handle accounting, respond to reviews, plan marketing, and cook the food. build.up should not add to this complexity. It should identify the ONE metric that matters most for each business type and ruthlessly simplify everything else.

**Specific build.up feature implication:** A "Kill List" feature that helps owners identify which activities drain time but generate no revenue, modeled on Steve Jobs' product matrix. Show owners: "You spend 4 hours/week on X, which generates 0 additional revenue. Here's what to cut."

## PATTERN 2: The Planned Deficit -- Losing Money on Purpose {#pattern-2}

### The Principle
**"Planned losses that build irreversible infrastructure advantages are not reckless -- they are the only path to dominance in winner-take-all markets."**

### Evidence Across Cases

**Coupang (2014-2022):** Coupang burned through trillions of won building its own logistics network (fulfillment centers + Coupang Friends delivery fleet) while competitors outsourced delivery. Investors called it reckless. Kim Bom-seok repeated one phrase: "planned deficit" (의도된 적자). SoftBank's Vision Fund invested massively. The result: 8 years after launching Rocket Delivery, Coupang achieved operating profitability and built a logistics moat no competitor could replicate.

**SpaceX (2006-2008):** Three consecutive rocket failures. Musk poured his remaining personal fortune ($100M) into a fourth launch attempt. "A fourth failure would have been absolutely game over." The September 28, 2008 success -- the first privately developed liquid-fueled rocket to reach orbit -- led directly to a $1.6B NASA contract on December 23, 2008. The planned deficit was not waste; it was R&D that created an entirely new industry.

**Market Kurly (2015-present):** Launched dawn delivery (새벽배송) knowing it would be unprofitable for years. The investment went into cold-chain logistics, data-driven demand prediction (the "data farm" team), and rigorous product curation where every item is taste-tested by staff. The infrastructure -- predicting weekly milk consumption, anticipating sports-event snack demand -- became the moat.

**Shopify (2006-2012):** Operated at a loss while building an open API and app store ecosystem. When the ecosystem launched, revenue quadrupled within months. The planned deficit was investment in platform infrastructure that created switching costs.

### The Korean SMB Application
**Most Korean small business owners cannot afford planned deficits.** This is the fundamental asymmetry: what works for VC-backed startups is fatal for a restaurant owner with 5,000만원 in capital. build.up must help owners distinguish between "investment spending" (which builds future capacity) and "hemorrhage spending" (which just delays death). The key question build.up should help owners answer: "Is this loss building something that will be worth more tomorrow, or am I just bleeding?"

**Specific build.up feature implication:** A "Spending Classifier" that categorizes each expense as either (a) capacity-building investment or (b) operational cost, with trend analysis showing whether each category is growing or shrinking relative to revenue.

## PATTERN 3: The Pivot -- When Your Failure Contains Your Fortune {#pattern-3}

### The Principle
**"The most valuable thing in a failed project is rarely the project itself -- it is the tool you built to make the project work."**

### Evidence Across Cases

**Slack ($27.7B):** Stewart Butterfield built a game called Glitch that nobody wanted to play. But the internal communication tool his team built to coordinate game development was extraordinary. The pivot from game to enterprise communication took 18 months. The critical insight: Butterfield had 3.5 years of "practice" using the tool with his own team before launching it publicly, giving him deep product-market fit understanding that competitors could not replicate.

**Nintendo (cards to games):** Founded in 1889 as a playing card company. When the card market saturated in the 1950s, Hiroshi Yamauchi tried taxis, love hotels, and instant rice before finding the path through toys (Ultra Hand, 1966), then electronic toys (Beam Gun, 1970), then arcade games (Donkey Kong, 1981), then home consoles (Famicom/NES, 1983). Each "failure" taught Nintendo something about entertainment that informed the next attempt.

**Toss (토스):** Founder Lee Seung-gun failed 8 times before creating the financial super-app. Each failure -- including a sonic communication-based SNS killed by Facebook, and a domestic helper platform killed by legal constraints -- taught him about mobile UX, payment friction, and regulatory navigation. These accumulated lessons became Toss's competitive advantage.

**Marvel (bankruptcy to $4.24B acquisition):** After 1996 bankruptcy, Marvel realized its most valuable asset was not comics but characters. The radical decision in 2003: stop licensing characters to other studios and create Marvel Studios to produce its own films. They secured a $525M loan from Merrill Lynch backed by the rights to Iron Man, Captain America, Thor, and The Avengers. Iron Man (2008) launched the MCU, which has now grossed $31B+ globally.

### The Korean SMB Application
**Korean small business owners rarely pivot -- they close.** The 2024 data shows 1,008,282 business closures, the highest ever recorded. Many of these businesses had accumulated valuable assets (customer relationships, operational knowledge, supplier networks, location awareness) that were destroyed upon closure. build.up should help owners see what transferable assets they have built, even in a failing business.

**Specific build.up feature implication:** A "Pivot Analyzer" that, when a business is struggling, surfaces: (1) the owner's most valuable accumulated skills, (2) adjacent business models that leverage those skills, and (3) case studies of similar pivots. Example: "You've built expertise in cold-chain logistics for your bakery. Here are 3 business models that leverage cold-chain expertise with higher margins."

## PATTERN 4: Developer/User Obsession Over Competitor Obsession {#pattern-4}

### The Principle
**"The companies that win are not the ones watching competitors -- they are the ones staring at users so intensely that they see needs the users themselves cannot articulate."**

### Evidence Across Cases

**Stripe vs. PayPal:** PayPal had a decade head start and massive scale. Stripe won by being obsessed with developers, not with PayPal. They offered 7 lines of code for integration (vs. weeks of PayPal setup). They priced at the premium end, forcing themselves to justify the price through product quality. They won PayPal's own founders (Thiel, Musk) as investors by articulating the original PayPal vision better than PayPal itself had executed it.

**Zoom vs. Skype:** Skype had a 10-year head start and Microsoft's backing. Zoom won by obsessing over one thing: making video calls frictionless. No account needed. No downloads. No pre-meeting updates. While Skype added features (ads, redesigns, chat, file sharing), Zoom perfected the core experience. Result: Zoom went from 10M to 300M daily users in 5 months during COVID, while Skype shrank to 0.72% market share.

**Canva vs. Adobe:** Adobe had 40 years of dominance. Canva founder Melanie Perkins, while teaching design at university, noticed that existing tools were intimidating for non-designers. She focused on the 99% of people who needed design but couldn't use Photoshop. After 100+ investor rejections, Canva launched in 2013 with a waitlist of 50,000. By focusing on the user Adobe ignored, Canva reached $40B valuation and 135M+ users.

**Figma vs. Adobe (again):** Figma made design collaborative and browser-based. The key insight was cross-side network effects: designers pull in developers to inspect files, developers pull in PMs to comment, PMs pull in marketers. Each new role added to the canvas increased gravitational pull. Adobe tried to acquire Figma for $20B but regulators blocked it -- Figma had become too important to be absorbed.

**Yanolja (야놀자):** Founder Lee Su-jin started as a motel cleaner. He didn't study the hotel industry from McKinsey reports -- he understood it from scrubbing floors. He built a community for accommodation workers before building a booking platform. This ground-level understanding of both operators and customers became Yanolja's data advantage, driving it to unicorn status with a $1B+ valuation.

### The Korean SMB Application
**Korean small business owners are drowning in competitor anxiety.** "The restaurant across the street lowered prices." "A new cafe opened nearby." build.up should redirect this energy from competitor-watching to customer-understanding. The most powerful feature would not be competitor analysis but customer behavior analysis.

**Specific build.up feature implication:** A "Customer Insight Radar" that tracks: (1) what time customers come, (2) what they buy together, (3) how weather/events affect demand, (4) which customers are at risk of churning (haven't visited in X days). This mirrors Market Kurly's "data farm" approach but scaled down for a single store.

## PATTERN 5: The Moat -- Build What Cannot Be Copied Overnight {#pattern-5}

### The Principle
**"The key to investing is determining the competitive advantage of any given company and, above all, the durability of that advantage." -- Warren Buffett**

### Types of Moats (with examples)

| Moat Type | Definition | Example | Korean SMB Equivalent |
|-----------|-----------|---------|----------------------|
| **Network Effects** | Product gets better as more people use it | Figma (designers + developers + PMs), Slack | A restaurant's regular customer community that brings friends |
| **Switching Costs** | Painful to leave once you're in | Shopify (store data, integrations, habits), Stripe (payment infrastructure) | Years of customer relationship data, supplier contracts |
| **Brand** | Trust that takes years to build | Starbucks (the "third place"), Apple ("Think Different") | Neighborhood reputation, word-of-mouth |
| **Cost Advantage** | Can do it cheaper due to scale/efficiency | Coupang (own logistics), Samsung (semiconductor fabs) | Bulk purchasing relationships, efficient kitchen layout |
| **Data** | Accumulated information that improves decisions | Yanolja (booking patterns), Market Kurly (demand prediction) | Knowledge of what sells on rainy Tuesdays in your neighborhood |
| **Regulatory/Legal** | Licenses or permits that limit competition | Franchise rights, special permits | Food safety certifications, location-specific permits |

### Peter Thiel's Monopoly Framework
Thiel argues that competition destroys profits. The goal is not to compete -- it is to build something so unique that you have no direct competitors. Start with a tiny market you can dominate completely, then expand concentrically. "Every startup should start with a very small market. The perfect target market is a small group of particular people concentrated together and served by few or no competitors."

### The Korean SMB Application
**Most Korean small businesses have no moat.** There are 6.9 million self-employed people in Korea, and most compete purely on price in commoditized markets. The typical chicken restaurant has no switching costs, no network effects, no data advantage, and no brand beyond the franchise name.

build.up's mission should be to help every small business build at least ONE moat, even a small one. The most accessible moat for a small business is **data** -- knowing your customers and your operations better than anyone else.

**Specific build.up feature implication:** A "Moat Score" dashboard showing: (1) Customer loyalty (% repeat customers), (2) Data depth (how much operational data you've accumulated), (3) Uniqueness (menu items or services competitors don't have), (4) Location advantage assessment. Each score comes with specific actions to strengthen it.

## PATTERN 6: Start Impossibly Small, Then Expand Deliberately {#pattern-6}

### The Principle
**"It's much better to first make a product a small number of users love than a product a large number of users like." -- Sam Altman**

### Evidence Across Cases

**Stripe:** Started by personally visiting potential customers and installing the integration for them. Patrick Collison would literally sit next to developers and integrate Stripe into their codebase. This "Collison Installation" became legendary -- the opposite of "build it and they will come."

**Databricks:** From 2009 to 2012, nobody would pay attention to Apache Spark. The team broke the world record for sorting a petabyte of data, which finally got attention. Only then did they build the commercial platform. They started with a single, impossibly narrow use case (big data processing) and expanded to become a $134B AI platform.

**Yanolja:** Started as an internet cafe (community forum) for motel workers -- an audience of perhaps a few thousand people in all of Korea. This tiny, overlooked community became the foundation for a travel unicorn.

**Canva:** Started with high school yearbooks in Australia. Melanie Perkins and Cliff Obrecht built Fusion Books, a yearbook customization tool, as a testing ground. Only after proving the concept in this minuscule market did they expand to general design.

### Sam Altman's Startup Playbook Rules
1. First make something a small number of users love
2. Recruit initial users one at a time
3. Build things users ask for
4. Don't do a "big press launch" (they almost never work)
5. Growth solves all problems; lack of growth is not solvable by anything but growth

### The Korean SMB Application
**Korean entrepreneurs often start too big.** They sign expensive leases, hire staff, and build out full menus before validating demand. The 2024 data shows 40% of small businesses close within 3 years, and 22% within the first year. The median business survives only 6.5 years.

build.up should encourage the "minimum viable store" approach: start with a smaller space, a limited menu, and a pop-up or delivery model to validate demand before committing to a full build-out.

**Specific build.up feature implication:** A "Pre-Launch Validator" that walks first-time entrepreneurs through: (1) Customer interview templates (adapted for Korean market), (2) Minimum viable cost calculator, (3) Demand validation checklist (pre-orders, pop-up test, social media interest gauge), (4) A "break-even countdown" showing how many days of operations their capital can sustain.

## PATTERN 7: Crisis as Catalyst -- The Companies That Used Near-Death to Transform {#pattern-7}

### The Principle
**"The company that survives a crisis is never the same company that entered it -- it is a better one, or it is dead."**

### The Transformation Matrix

| Company | Crisis | Near-Death Metric | Key Decision | Outcome |
|---------|--------|-------------------|--------------|---------|
| **SpaceX** | 3 failed launches (2006-2008) | $0 in reserve after 3rd failure | Bet everything on 4th launch | $1.6B NASA contract, now most valuable private company |
| **Apple** | 90 days from bankruptcy (1997) | -$1B annual loss | Kill 70% of products, accept $150M from Microsoft | Most valuable company in history |
| **LEGO** | -$1M/day losses (2003) | $800M debt | Cut SKUs by 46%, sell theme parks, return to the brick | World's largest toy company |
| **Marvel** | Chapter 11 bankruptcy (1996) | Stock at $2.38 (down from $35.75) | Create own movie studio, bet on characters as IP | $4.24B Disney acquisition, MCU = $31B+ revenue |
| **Samsung** | IMF crisis (1997) | Debt-to-equity ratio >530% | Cut 50,000 employees, reduce from 65 to 45 affiliates | Global #1 in memory chips, smartphones |
| **Starbucks** | 28% profit decline (2008) | 900 store closures, 6,700 layoffs | Return to craft, retrain all baristas, close unprofitable stores | Profits 3x'd from $315M to $945M in 2 years |
| **NVIDIA** | Wrong architecture for Sega (1996) | 6 months to bankruptcy | Admit mistake to Sega, ask for payment anyway, redesign from scratch | $3T+ company, AI computing monopoly |

### Jensen Huang's Three Near-Death Lessons
1. **The Sega Crisis:** Admit your mistake immediately and ask for help with humility. The Sega CEO paid NVIDIA in full despite canceling the contract, giving them 6 months to survive.
2. **The CUDA Bet:** Invest in a vision even when the market doesn't understand it yet. CUDA lost money initially but created the foundation for AI computing.
3. **The Mobile Exit:** Know when to quit. NVIDIA left the mobile chip market to focus on robotics and self-driving, which required the courage to abandon a massive market.

### The Korean SMB Application
**Korean small business owners experience crisis constantly** -- rising minimum wages, rent increases, new competitors, economic downturns. The 2024 closure data shows the top reason is "revenue decline / profitability deterioration" (86.7%), driven by: customer reduction due to weak domestic demand (52.2%), rising labor costs (49.4%), rising material costs (46.0%), and rising fixed costs like rent (44.6%).

The difference between businesses that survive and those that die is not the absence of crisis but the response to it. build.up should be a "crisis response system" that activates when key metrics deteriorate.

**Specific build.up feature implication:** A "Crisis Protocol" system that:
1. Detects early warning signs (3 consecutive weeks of revenue decline, margin compression below threshold)
2. Immediately surfaces the specific crisis type (demand problem vs. cost problem vs. cash flow problem)
3. Provides a step-by-step response playbook tailored to the crisis type
4. Shows case studies of similar businesses that survived the same crisis
5. Connects owners to emergency resources (government programs, emergency loans, mentors)

## PATTERN 8: Premature Scaling -- The #1 Killer of Promising Businesses {#pattern-8}

### The Principle
**"Premature scaling is the most common cause of startup death. It is spending money to grow a business that hasn't yet proven it deserves to grow."**

### Evidence Across Cases

**WeMakePrice/TMON (위메프/티몬) -- The Catastrophic Failure:**
The 2024 TMON/WeMakePrice payment settlement crisis is the most devastating recent Korean startup failure. Parent company Qoo10 aggressively acquired WeMakePrice, TMON, Interpark, AK Mall, and Wish to inflate external appearance for a NASDAQ IPO of subsidiary Qoo10 Express. The result: 1 trillion+ won in unpaid merchant settlements. TMON was already in complete capital erosion (-6,386억원 equity). CEO Ku Young-bae admitted using merchant settlement funds to finance the Wish acquisition. This is premature scaling in its most toxic form: acquiring companies to look big rather than building genuine value.

**Zigbang (직방) -- Acquisition Overreach:**
After becoming Korea's #1 real estate app with 35M+ downloads, Zigbang went on an acquisition spree: Hogaengnono, Wooju, Sugarhill, Samsung SDS Home IoT division. Operating losses exploded from 82억원 (2021) to 371억원 (2022). Subsidiary loans ballooned from 105억원 to 852억원. Meanwhile, Danggeun Market ate their market share by offering free real estate listings. The acquisitions didn't strengthen the core product -- they distracted from it.

**CB Insights 2024 Data -- The Numbers:**
- 431 VC-backed companies died since 2023, having raised a combined $17.5B
- Median company raised $11M before dying
- Median time from last fundraise to death: 22 months
- 70% ran out of capital (immediate cause)
- 43% had poor product-market fit (root cause)
- 29% had bad timing
- 19% had unsustainable unit economics

### Paul Graham's 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups (Most Relevant)
1. **Not making something users want** -- the single most common cause
2. **Single founder** -- successful startups typically have 2+ cofounders
3. **Raising too much money** -- changes incentives, encourages waste
4. **Poor location** -- startup ecosystems matter
5. **Fights between founders** -- cofounder breakups are a leading cause of death
6. **Half-hearted effort** -- startups require total commitment
7. **Choosing an obscure niche** -- too small to matter
8. **Not wanting to get your hands dirty** -- "do things that don't scale"

### First-Time Founder Mistakes (Harvard Innovation Labs)
- **Building without validation:** 35% of failures cite "no market need"
- **Mistaking polite interest for purchase intent:** Friends say "great idea" but never buy
- **Incorporating too early:** Creates obligations before value is proven
- **Premature scaling:** Hiring before product-market fit
- **No business model:** Even great products need sustainable economics
- **Rushing to raise funding:** Funding is fuel for scaling what works, not a substitute for finding what works

### The Korean SMB Application
**Korean small business premature scaling looks different from startup premature scaling.** It is: signing a 5-year lease before testing the location with a pop-up; hiring 3 staff members before knowing if demand justifies it; building out an elaborate interior before the first customer walks in; buying expensive equipment for a menu that hasn't been validated.

**Specific build.up feature implication:** A "Scale Readiness Score" that gates expansion decisions:
- Want to hire? Score must show consistent demand exceeding current capacity for 3+ months
- Want to expand space? Score must show geographic demand data supporting the new location
- Want to add menu items? Score must show customer request data justifying the addition
- Each "gate" comes with a warning: "X% of businesses that expanded at your current metrics failed within 12 months"

## PATTERN 9: The Founder's Character -- Resilience Over Brilliance {#pattern-9}

### The Principle
**"The most important characteristics of great founders are unstoppability, determination, formidability, and resourcefulness." -- Sam Altman**

### Evidence Across Cases

**Jensen Huang (NVIDIA):** Sent to a boarding school in one of America's poorest areas at age 9 as an immigrant. Credits extreme childhood hardship with building stress tolerance. "I think best under adversity." This mindset enabled navigating 3 near-death experiences over 30 years.

**Lee Su-jin (Yanolja):** Started as a motel cleaner with 5,000만원 in capital. Built a community forum for accommodation workers before anyone saw opportunity in the budget hotel market. Didn't come from privilege -- came from the industry floor.

**Lee Seung-gun (Toss):** Failed 8 times before creating Korea's most important fintech company. Each failure taught him something specific. He didn't have 8 ideas -- he had 1 vision (financial accessibility) tested through 8 iterations.

**Melanie Perkins (Canva):** Rejected by 100+ investors. Pitched the idea while teaching university design classes in Perth, Australia -- about as far from Silicon Valley as possible. Took 3 years from first pitch to first funding.

**Elon Musk (SpaceX/Tesla, 2008):** Simultaneously facing Tesla and SpaceX bankruptcy while going through a divorce. Had to choose which company to save with his last $40M. Chose to split it. Both survived by weeks.

**Patrick Collison (Stripe):** At 19, pitched PayPal's own founders that PayPal was broken. "It's a little impetuous to go to PayPal founders and say payments on the internet are totally broken." The audacity was the point -- it demonstrated conviction.

### Patrick Collison's Operating Principles
- Treat intellectual ambition as core -- organizational failures are ultimately failures of thinking
- Favor written communication (memos over presentations) to force clarity of thought
- Hire for intellectual honesty and bias toward action, not credentials
- Before product-market fit, prioritize "high throughput qualitative feedback" over metrics
- Core values: "Think rigorously," "Trust, but verify," "We haven't won yet"

### The Korean SMB Application
**Korean small business owners face a resilience crisis.** The culture of "saving face" (체면) makes failure feel catastrophic rather than educational. The 소상공인 closure rate exceeds 100만/year, and many owners experience depression, family stress, and social shame upon closure.

build.up can't give owners resilience directly, but it can: (1) normalize failure through stories, (2) provide early warning systems that prevent unnecessary failure, (3) create a community of owners who share struggles openly, and (4) reframe closure as "strategic exit" rather than "personal failure."

**Specific build.up feature implication:** A "Founder's Resilience" section with:
- Weekly stories of Korean entrepreneurs who failed and recovered (이승건, etc.)
- An anonymous community forum for sharing struggles (modeled on how Yanolja started as a community)
- Mental health check-ins tied to business stress indicators
- "Exit Planning" tools that help owners close gracefully when the data says it's time, preserving capital and dignity for the next attempt

## SYNTHESIS: The 12 Principles build.up Must Embody {#synthesis}

Drawing from all 30+ case studies, here are the universal principles that should inform every feature, every screen, and every notification in build.up:

### Principle 1: SIMPLIFY RUTHLESSLY
**Source:** Apple (4 products), LEGO (cut 46% of SKUs), Samsung (65 to 45 affiliates)
**build.up rule:** Every screen should do ONE thing. Never show more than 3 metrics at once. If a feature doesn't help the owner make money or save money, kill it.

### Principle 2: MEASURE WHAT MATTERS
**Source:** CB Insights (43% failed due to no product-market fit), Korean closure data (86.7% cite revenue decline)
**build.up rule:** Revenue, margin, and cash runway are the three numbers that matter. Everything else is vanity. Show these three numbers on every login screen.

### Principle 3: WARN EARLY
**Source:** SpaceX (4th launch was last chance), Marvel (bankruptcy came after years of ignoring decline)
**build.up rule:** Detect trouble 90 days before it becomes fatal. "Your revenue has declined for 3 consecutive weeks. Here's what businesses like yours did at this point."

### Principle 4: VALIDATE BEFORE COMMITTING
**Source:** Sam Altman (recruit users one at a time), Canva (100+ rejections before funding), Paul Graham (make something users want)
**build.up rule:** Before any major investment (new lease, new hire, new equipment), show the owner data on whether the investment is justified. "Similar businesses in your area with your revenue levels that hired a 3rd employee saw X outcome."

### Principle 5: BUILD ONE MOAT
**Source:** Buffett (economic moat), Thiel (monopoly), Stripe (developer loyalty), Coupang (logistics)
**build.up rule:** Help every business identify and build at least one competitive advantage: customer loyalty, location knowledge, unique product, data-driven operations, or community.

### Principle 6: START SMALL, PROVE, THEN SCALE
**Source:** Databricks (tiny to $134B), Yanolja (motel forum to unicorn), Thiel (dominate small market first)
**build.up rule:** Gate scaling decisions behind data. "You've maintained 90%+ capacity for 12 consecutive weeks. Now is the time to consider expansion." Never encourage growth that the data doesn't support.

### Principle 7: LEARN FROM YOUR OWN DATA
**Source:** Market Kurly (data farm), Yanolja (booking patterns), Figma (usage analytics)
**build.up rule:** Every transaction, every customer visit, every inventory movement is a data point. Over time, build.up should know the business better than the owner does, surfacing insights like: "Your most profitable day is Wednesday. Your least profitable menu item is #7. Your best customer hasn't visited in 14 days."

### Principle 8: NORMALIZE FAILURE
**Source:** Toss (8 failures), Nintendo (taxis, hotels, rice before games), Slack (failed game)
**build.up rule:** Show failure stories alongside success stories. Frame closure as "strategic repositioning" when the data supports it. Provide exit planning tools that preserve capital for the next attempt.

### Principle 9: CRISIS IS A FEATURE, NOT A BUG
**Source:** Every turnaround story (Apple, LEGO, Samsung, Starbucks, SpaceX, Marvel, NVIDIA)
**build.up rule:** When metrics indicate crisis, don't hide it. Activate "Crisis Mode" with a clear protocol: diagnose (demand vs. cost vs. cash flow problem), prescribe (specific actions), connect (resources and mentors), and track recovery.

### Principle 10: THE USER IS NOT A METRIC -- THEY ARE A PERSON
**Source:** Starbucks (emotional connection), Yanolja (started from cleaning floors), Zoom (frictionless experience)
**build.up rule:** Korean small business owners are stressed, overworked, and often scared. Every interaction should reduce anxiety, not increase it. The tone should be that of a trusted 형/누나/선배 who has been through it, not a cold analytics dashboard.

### Principle 11: INFRASTRUCTURE BEATS FEATURES
**Source:** Coupang (built logistics), Shopify (built ecosystem), Stripe (built payment rails), Databricks (built data platform)
**build.up rule:** build.up's long-term moat is not any single feature but the accumulated operational data of thousands of Korean small businesses. This data -- properly anonymized and aggregated -- becomes the infrastructure that makes every individual recommendation smarter. The more businesses use build.up, the better build.up becomes for each business.

### Principle 12: OBSESS OVER THE USER, NOT THE COMPETITION
**Source:** Stripe (developer obsession), Zoom (user obsession), Canva (non-designer obsession), Figma (collaboration obsession), Patrick Collison ("qualitative feedback over metrics" pre-PMF)
**build.up rule:** Spend the first year talking to 1,000 Korean small business owners. Not surveying them -- talking to them. Sitting in their stores. Understanding their daily rhythm. The product roadmap should come from their mouths, not from competitive analysis.
