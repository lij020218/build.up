# Startup Founding Stage: Best Advice from Top Sources

> Compiled 2026-04-02 | Focused on actionable advice for a tech startup founder in Korea

---

## 1. Y COMBINATOR — Paul Graham & Partners

### 1A. Defining the Problem

**The #1 Rule: Work on problems you have yourself.**

Paul Graham, "How to Get Startup Ideas" (2012):

> "The way to get startup ideas is not to try to think of startup ideas. It's to look for problems, preferably problems you have yourself."

> "The very best startup ideas tend to have three things in common: they're something the founders themselves want, that they themselves can build, and that few others realize are worth doing. Microsoft, Apple, Yahoo, Google, and Facebook all began this way."

**The "Sitcom Startup" trap:**

> "At YC we call these 'made-up' or 'sitcom' startup ideas. Imagine one of the characters on a TV show was starting a startup. The writers would have to invent something for it to do. But coming up with good startup ideas is hard... so the writers would come up with an idea that sounded plausible, but was actually bad."

**The Well vs. the Broad Hole:**

> "You can either build something a large number of people want a small amount, or something a small number of people want a large amount. Choose the latter."

> "When you have an idea for a startup, ask yourself: who wants this right now? Who wants this so much that they'll use it even when it's a crappy version one made by a two-person startup they've never heard of? If you can't answer that, the idea is probably bad."

**The key formula:**

> "Live in the future, then build what's missing." (Paul Buchheit, cited by Graham)

---

### 1B. Co-Founder Selection and Equity Split

**Paul Graham, "Before the Startup" (2014):**

> "You can, however, trust your instincts about people... If someone seems slippery, or bogus, or a jerk, don't ignore it. This is one case where it pays to be self-indulgent. Work with people you genuinely like, and you've known long enough to be sure."

**Michael Seibel (YC CEO), "How to Split Equity Among Founders" (2024):**

> "It takes 7 to 10 years to build a company of great value. Small variations in year one do not justify massively different founder equity splits in year 2-10."

> "More equity = more motivation. Almost all startups fail. The more motivated the founders, the higher the chance of success."

> "Startups are about execution, not about ideas. Dramatically unequal founder equity splits often give undue preference to the co-founder who initially came up with the idea."

**Seibel's core recommendation: Equal or near-equal splits.** If you aren't willing to give your partner an equal share, perhaps you're choosing the wrong partner. Unequal splits signal to investors that a co-founder "isn't very good or isn't going to be very impactful."

**Mistakes to avoid:**
- Splitting based on who had the idea
- Splitting based on who started working first
- Splitting based on prior salary contributions
- Splitting based on age/experience differences

**Paul Graham's Equity Equation:**

> "You should give up n% of your company if what you trade it for improves your average outcome enough that the (100 - n)% you have left is worth more than the whole company was before."

> "You should always feel richer after trading equity."

### 1C. When to Incorporate vs. Just Build

**YC's practical stance (from Clerky, YC's recommended incorporation tool):**

There is NO universally defined "too early" moment. Incorporation timing depends on specific triggers:

| Trigger | When to Incorporate |
|---------|-------------------|
| Multiple founders | To formalize equity splits with vesting schedules and ensure IP belongs to the company |
| Fundraising | Nearly all investors require incorporation before investing |
| First hire | To issue equity compensation and ensure work products are owned by the corporation |
| First customer | Once liability risk increases; personal liability protection needed |
| Entering contracts | To bind the corporation, not individuals |

**The key insight: Build and validate FIRST.** Many of the most successful companies started as informal projects and incorporated only when external triggers required it (see Section 4 below).

### 1D. "Do Things That Don't Scale" Philosophy

Paul Graham, "Do Things That Don't Scale" (2013):

> "Actually startups take off because the founders make them take off. There may be a handful that just grew by themselves, but usually it takes some sort of push to get them going."

**The "Collison Installation" (Stripe):**

> "At YC we use the term 'Collison installation' for the technique they invented. More diffident founders ask 'Will you try our beta?' and if the answer is yes, they say 'Great, we'll send you a link.' But the Collison brothers weren't going to wait. When anyone agreed to try Stripe they'd say 'Right then, give me your laptop' and set them up on the spot."

**Airbnb's heroic measures:**

> "In Airbnb's case, these consisted of going door to door in New York, recruiting new users and helping existing ones improve their listings. When I remember the Airbnbs during YC, I picture them with rolly bags, because when they showed up for Tuesday dinners they'd always just flown back from somewhere."

**Wufoo's hand-written notes:**

> "For as long as they could (which turned out to be surprisingly long), Wufoo sent each new user a hand-written thank you note."

**The fragility insight:**

> "Airbnb now seems like an unstoppable juggernaut, but early on it was so fragile that about 30 days of going out and engaging in person with users made the difference between success and failure."

> "The question to ask about an early stage startup is not 'is this company taking over the world?' but 'how big could this company get if the founders did the right things?'"

**Founder's biggest enemy — dismissing their own startup:**

> "I often have to encourage founders who don't see the full potential of what they're building. Even Bill Gates made that mistake. He returned to Harvard for the fall semester after starting Microsoft."

### 1E. YC on "Playing House" (Don't Optimize for Looking Like a Startup)

Paul Graham, "Before the Startup" (2014):

> "Another of the characteristic mistakes of young founders is to go through the motions of starting a startup. They make up some plausible-sounding idea, raise money at a good valuation, rent a cool office, hire a bunch of people... while imitating all the outward forms of a startup they have neglected the one thing that's actually essential: making something people want."

> "The way to succeed in a startup is not to be an expert on startups, but to be an expert on your users and the problem you're solving for them. Mark Zuckerberg didn't succeed because he was an expert on startups. He succeeded despite being a complete noob at startups, because he understood his users really well."

> "If I met an undergrad who knew all about convertible notes and employee agreements and (God forbid) class FF stock, I wouldn't think 'here is someone who is way ahead of their peers.' It would set off alarms."

## 2. REID HOFFMAN / Blitzscaling & The Startup of You

### 2A. "If You're Not Embarrassed, You've Launched Too Late"

Reid Hoffman's most famous quote:

> **"If you're not embarrassed by the first version of your product, you've launched too late."**

Three principles behind this:
1. **Speed matters** — ship fast, learn fast
2. **Your assumptions are wrong** — you'll be embarrassed by how many wrong assumptions you made
3. **Delay kills feedback loops** — you lose out by delaying the onset of the customer feedback loop

**The LinkedIn example:** Just before LinkedIn launched, his team said they needed to build a "Contact Finder" feature first. Hoffman said to launch as planned and add it if users wanted it. **Seven years later, LinkedIn still hadn't launched that feature** — users never needed it.

### 2B. "Throw Yourself Off a Cliff"

Hoffman's metaphor for starting a company:

> **"You throw yourself off a cliff and assemble your airplane on the way down."**

Being resource-efficient lets you "glide" to minimize the rate of descent, giving you time to learn about your market, technology, and team before you hit the ground.

### 2C. When to Formalize Company Structure

Hoffman's framework distinguishes stages. Blitzscaling is NOT the right strategy from day one. It's for when you've found product-market fit and need to scale before competitors. In the founding stage, the priority is:
1. Validate the problem
2. Ship something (even if embarrassing)
3. Find product-market fit
4. THEN worry about structure, scaling, and formalization

**Important caveat on the embarrassment quote:**

> Hoffman clarified: "He did not write 'If you're not indicted by the first version'... If you launch so fast that your product generates lawsuits, alienates users, or burns through resources without gain, you launched too soon."

## 3. BEN HOROWITZ / The Hard Thing About Hard Things

### 3A. Making Decisions with Incomplete Information

> **"Every decision that a CEO makes is based on incomplete information. At the time of any given decision, the CEO will generally have less than 10 percent of the information typically present in the post hoc Harvard Business School case study."**

> "As a result, the CEO must have the courage to bet the company on a direction even though she does not know if the direction is right."

> **"The ability to focus and make the best move when there are no good moves."** — Horowitz's definition of a key CEO skill

### 3B. Wartime vs. Peacetime CEO

> "Peacetime CEO spends time defining the culture. **Wartime CEO lets the war define the culture.**"

> "Peacetime CEO knows that proper protocol leads to winning. **Wartime CEO violates protocol in order to win.**"

> "Peacetime CEO thinks of the competition as other ships in a big ocean. **Wartime CEO thinks the competition is sneaking into her house and trying to kidnap her children.**"

**For early-stage founders:** You are always in wartime. Act accordingly.

### 3C. Culture — A Nuanced View

> **"The world is full of bankrupt companies with world-class cultures. Culture does not make a company."**

> "Every time you make the hard, correct decision you become a bit more courageous, and every time you make the easy, wrong decision you become a bit more cowardly."

Culture is defined by your actions, not your stated values. Every hard decision you make in the early days IS your culture being created.

### 3D. The Struggle

> "The Struggle is when you wonder why you started the company in the first place. The Struggle is when people ask you why you don't quit and you don't know the answer."

Horowitz's honest admission: "I was probably not the only one struggling." The isolation of leadership is a universal experience — the important thing is to not face it alone and to keep making decisions even when everything feels impossible.

## 4. REAL STARTUP STORIES: From Nothing to Everything

### 4A. Facebook — Dorm Room, No Corporation

**Timeline:**
- **February 4, 2004:** Mark Zuckerberg launched "thefacebook" from his Harvard dorm room (Kirkland House). Single server, $85/month hosting.
- **First month:** 1,200 Harvard students signed up
- **Early 2004:** Quick Florida LLC formed (cheap, fast, just to have a legal entity for basic operations)
- **July 29, 2004:** Incorporated as "TheFacebook, Inc." in Delaware — only after moving to Palo Alto and getting serious about growth
- **April 2005:** First outside investment — $500,000 from Peter Thiel
- **2005:** $12.7M Series A from Accel Partners

**Key lesson:** Zuckerberg coded and launched FIRST. The LLC came weeks/months later as a practical necessity. The Delaware corporation came 6 months after launch. The product existed and had thousands of users before any formal legal structure.

Paul Graham on Facebook's early strategy:

> "At first it was just for Harvard students. In that form it only had a potential market of a few thousand people, but because they felt it was really for them, a critical mass of them signed up."

### 4B. Apple — Garage and a Typed Partnership Agreement

**Timeline:**
- **April 1, 1976:** Steve Jobs (21), Steve Wozniak (25), and Ron Wayne met in Wayne's apartment
- Wayne typed up a partnership agreement on the spot (NOT a napkin — but a simple, typed document)
- **Equity split:** 45% Jobs, 45% Wozniak, 10% Wayne
- **Initial capital:** Jobs sold his VW Bus, Wozniak sold his HP-65 calculator — together raised $1,300
- **11 days later:** Ron Wayne filed a statement of withdrawal (giving up what would have been ~$300 billion)
- Apple started production in the Jobs family garage

**Key lesson:** Apple started as a PARTNERSHIP, not a corporation. The formal incorporation came later. The product (Apple I) came before the legal structure. The founding document was typed in someone's apartment, not drafted by lawyers.

### 4C. Stripe — "7 Lines of Code" and the Collison Installation

**Timeline:**
- **2007:** Patrick Collison (19) and John Collison (17) founded their first company, Shuppa/Auctomatic
- **2008:** Sold Auctomatic for $5 million — millionaires at 19 and 17
- **2010:** Dropped out of college, launched Stripe with seed funding from YC ($20-30K range)
- **Key innovation:** Reduced payment integration from weeks of work to 7 lines of copy-paste code

**The "Collison Installation" (do things that don't scale):**
During YC, the Collisons did NOT launch publicly. Instead they:
- Went to other YC companies' offices personally
- Offered to handle payments integration on the spot
- When someone agreed, they'd say "give me your laptop" and set it up immediately
- Built an elite private beta group of YC startups as first users

**Key lesson:** Product existed before formal go-to-market. Manual, unscalable user acquisition built the foundation. The code was the company — not the legal entity.

### 4D. Airbnb — From Air Mattresses to Cereal Boxes to $100B

**Timeline:**
- **2007:** Brian Chesky and Joe Gebbia can't afford rent in San Francisco. They buy 3 air mattresses and host conference attendees at $80/night each.
- **2008:** Targeted the Democratic National Convention in Denver. Usage spiked but didn't sustain.
- **The Cereal Pivot:** Desperate for money, they designed "Obama O's: The Breakfast of Change" and "Cap'n McCain's: A Maverick in Every Bite" cereal boxes. Sold each for $40, hand-numbered and hand-crafted. **Made $30,000 selling cereal.**
- **Late 2008:** Paul Graham was skeptical of the business model but was won over by the cereal box hustle, which proved the founders' determination.
- **Early 2009:** Accepted into Y Combinator. $20,000 seed money for 6% equity.
- During YC, they commuted from Mountain View to NYC, met EVERY host, lived with hosts, wrote the first reviews, helped take photos.

**Key lesson:** No legal entity when they started renting air mattresses. No legal entity when they sold cereal boxes. They proved demand, proved hustle, proved resilience — THEN formalized.

### 4E. Korean Startups

#### Toss (토스) — Lee Seung-gun (이승건)

- **Background:** Dentist at a Samsung-owned hospital. Left at ~29-30 to start a tech company.
- **Investment:** 150 million won (~$105,000) of personal savings
- **First 5 years:** Failed 8 times. Ideas included social media platforms and voting apps — he calls them "rubbish ideas"
- **Rock bottom:** Bank account had 20,000 won ($14). Asked employees' families to allow workers to continue without pay.
- **The breakthrough:** A money transfer service. "Toss was another stupid idea, but we were crazy enough to do it, because we had nothing to lose."
- **Today:** Viva Republica is worth ~$7 billion. Toss is Korea's leading fintech super app (banking, insurance, stock trading, payments).

**Key lesson:** 8 failures before finding what worked. Personal savings depleted. Near-bankruptcy. The winning idea seemed just as "stupid" as the failed ones — the difference was persistence and timing.

#### Coupang (쿠팡) — Bom Kim (김범석)

- **Background:** Harvard College graduate, HBS dropout (left in 2nd semester)
- **2010:** Founded Coupang in Seoul, initially as a Groupon-style daily deals business
- **Pivoted:** Quickly shifted to an eBay-inspired third-party marketplace
- **Within 3 years:** Crossed $1 billion in sales
- **The Radical Pivot:** The weekend before his IPO, Kim pulled out and spent a year transforming Coupang into an end-to-end e-commerce platform with its own UPS-style logistics ("Rocket Delivery")
- **2021:** IPO'd on NYSE. Today one of Korea's most valuable companies.

**Key lesson:** Started with someone else's model (Groupon clone), pivoted fast, was willing to cancel his OWN IPO to rebuild the business from scratch.

#### Baemin (배달의민족) — Kim Bong-jin (김봉진)

- **Background:** Interior design student, then web designer at companies like NHN (now Naver). Grew up on a small island, couldn't afford a bedroom during high school ("guests had to leave our family diner so I could sleep").
- **2010:** Started by literally walking around Gangnam, Seoul, picking up restaurant flyers from the ground and scanning them
- **June 2010:** First app released
- **March 2011:** Formally founded Woowa Brothers
- **Growth:** Reached 70% market share in Korean food delivery
- **2019:** Sold to Germany's Delivery Hero for $4.3 billion

**Key lesson:** The app existed for 5 months before the company was formally founded. Started by scanning paper flyers off the ground — the most analog, unscalable beginning imaginable for what became Korea's dominant food delivery platform.

## 5. MODERN 2024-2026 TRENDS

### 5A. The Rise of Solo Founders & AI-First Startups

**Statistics:**
- Solo-founded startups rose from 17% (2015) to 35%+ (2024) of all new startups in the US
- 52.3% of successful startup exits were achieved by solo founders
- 44% of profitable SaaS products are now run by a single founder (doubled since 2018)
- Micro-SaaS market projected to grow from $15.7B (2024) to $59.6B (2030)
- Over 60% of Korean solo founders (1인 창업자) start as a side business while still employed

**Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei (May 2025):**
Predicted the first billion-dollar, one-person company would emerge by 2026, giving it 70-80% odds.

### 5B. Extreme Success Stories (2025-2026)

| Founder | Product | Result | Timeline |
|---------|---------|--------|----------|
| Maor Shlomo | Base44 | Sold to Wix for $80M | Built solo in Dec 2024, sold in June 2025. No co-founder, no seed round, no team. 250K users, profitable at acquisition. |
| Danny Postma | HeadshotPro | $3.6M ARR | Solo operation. Launched quickly, validated with ads. |
| Peter Steinberger | OpenClaw | Acquired by OpenAI | Built v1 on a Friday evening in 1 hour. 145K GitHub stars. Meta and OpenAI both bid. |
| Sarah Chen | AI Design Agency | $420K ARR in 8 months | ChatGPT + Canva + Zapier. 25 hours/week. |

### 5C. The Modern "Build First" Stack

With AI tools in 2026, a solo founder can achieve 3-5x productivity:
- **Payments:** Stripe
- **Auth:** Clerk or Firebase
- **Database:** Supabase or Firebase
- **AI:** OpenAI / Anthropic APIs
- **Email:** Resend
- **Deployment:** Vercel
- **Development:** Cursor / Claude Code

**Speed benchmark:** Ship an MVP in 2-6 weeks (or even 2 days for simple products).

### 5D. When Incorporation Actually Becomes Necessary

Based on consensus from Clerky (YC's recommendation), Stripe, Carta, and multiple legal sources:

**You MUST incorporate when:**
1. **You accept money from investors** — all investors require a legal entity
2. **You hire your first employee** — for IP ownership, liability, and equity grants
3. **You receive your first customer payment** — for liability protection and tax compliance
4. **You sign significant contracts** — to bind the company, not you personally
5. **You have multiple co-founders** — to formalize equity with vesting

**You can safely WAIT if:**
- You're still building the MVP solo
- You're validating the idea with potential users
- You're a solo founder with no outside money
- You haven't started charging customers yet

**Korea-specific note:** In Korea, 법인설립 (incorporation) is relatively simple and inexpensive through 정관 작성 and 등기, but the same triggers apply. Many Korean founders, including Baemin's Kim Bong-jin, released their product BEFORE formal company registration.

### 5E. Korea 2026 Startup Ecosystem Context

- Government committed 3.46 trillion won (~$2.6B) — the largest startup support budget in Korean history
- Venture investment reached 9.8 trillion won in the first three quarters of 2025
- **But:** The number of funded startups fell 9% YoY — capital is concentrating toward later-stage
- Hot sectors: AI, deep tech, biotech
- Ministry of SMEs and Startups (중소벤처기업부) launching programs supporting non-face-to-face entrepreneurs with up to 20 million won
- Evaluators prioritize **execution capability** over hypothetical market size
- **Demonstrable progress (prototype, pilot customers, revenue) dramatically improves approval odds** for government support programs

## 6. PETER THIEL / Zero to One

### 6A. The Secret — Your Unique Insight

> **"What important truth do few people agree with you on?"** — Thiel's contrarian interview question

> "Every great business is built around a secret that's hidden from the outside. A great company is a conspiracy to change the world."

> "There are two kinds of secrets: secrets of nature and secrets about people."

**Framework:** Your startup needs a "secret" — a non-obvious truth that you believe but most people don't. If your insight is obvious, the market is already being served. If it's genuinely secret, you have a real opportunity.

### 6B. Thiel's Law — Foundations Cannot Be Fixed

> **"A startup messed up at its foundation cannot be fixed."** — known as "Thiel's Law"

> "If you get the founding moment right, you can do more than create a valuable company: you can steer its distant future toward the creation of new things instead of the stewardship of inherited success."

The founding "lasts as long as a company is creating new things, and it ends when creation stops."

### 6C. Founding Team Dynamics

> **"Founders should share a prehistory before they start a company together — otherwise they're just rolling dice."**

> "Technical abilities and complementary skill sets matter, but how well the founders know each other and how well they work together matter just as much."

**Board rule:** "A board of three is ideal. Your board should never exceed five people, unless your company is publicly held."

**Commitment rule:** "You're either on the bus or off the bus." — Part-time employees and remote work create misalignment.

### 6D. Equity and Compensation Philosophy

> **"A company does better the less it pays the CEO."**

> "In no case should a CEO of an early-stage, venture-backed startup receive more than $150,000 per year in salary."

> "If a CEO collects $300,000 per year, he risks becoming more like a politician than a founder."

> **"Anyone who prefers owning a part of your company to being paid in cash reveals a preference for the long term."**

> "Giving everyone equal shares is usually a mistake: every individual has different talents and responsibilities as well as different opportunity costs."

> "Since it's impossible to achieve perfect fairness when distributing ownership, founders would do well to keep the details secret."

**Note:** This contradicts Michael Seibel's (YC) advice for equal splits. The resolution: **Seibel is talking about co-founder splits specifically (where near-equal is usually right); Thiel is talking about ALL equity including early employees (where different levels are appropriate).**

### 6E. The Recruiting Question

> "Why will the 20th talented person join your company when they can get paid way more at Google? They'll have to work way less hard at Google."

The answer can't just be money. It has to be mission, equity upside, and the opportunity to do meaningful work that wouldn't be possible at a big company.

## 7. ELON MUSK — First Principles Thinking

### 7A. The Framework

> **"I tend to approach things from a physics framework. Physics teaches you to reason from first principles rather than by analogy."**

His 3-step process:
1. **Identify your assumptions** about the problem
2. **Break the problem down** to its most fundamental truths
3. **Reason up from there** to create new solutions

### 7B. The Rocket Example (SpaceX, 2002)

Musk wanted to send a rocket to Mars. Aerospace manufacturers quoted $65 million per rocket.

> "What is a rocket made of? Aerospace-grade aluminum alloys, plus some titanium, copper, and carbon fiber. Then I asked, what is the value of those materials on the commodity market? It turned out that **the materials cost of a rocket was around two percent of the typical price.**"

Instead of buying a finished rocket, Musk founded SpaceX, bought raw materials cheaply, and built rockets himself. Within years, SpaceX cut launch costs by nearly 10x while still making a profit.

### 7C. The Battery Example (Tesla)

Industry pricing: $600 per kilowatt hour for battery packs.

> "What are the material constituents of the batteries? What is the spot market value of the material constituents? It's got cobalt, nickel, aluminum, carbon, some polymers for separation, and a steel can. Break that down on a material basis — if we bought that on the London Metal Exchange, what would each of those things cost? **It's $80/kWh.**"

> "So clearly, you just need to think of clever ways to take those materials and combine them into the shape of a battery cell."

**Result:** By 2023, battery pack prices dropped to $128/kWh industry-wide.

### 7D. Starting with the Hardest Problem

Musk's approach: identify the single hardest technical problem and attack it directly. For SpaceX, it was reusable rockets. For Tesla, it was battery costs. For Neuralink, it's the brain-computer interface.

If the hardest problem is unsolvable, the rest doesn't matter. If it IS solvable, everything else becomes easier by comparison.

### 7E. Application for Korean Tech Founders

First principles thinking applied to the Korean market means:
- Don't copy what Silicon Valley is doing (reasoning by analogy)
- Ask: What are the FUNDAMENTAL needs of Korean small business owners / consumers?
- Break down the cost/complexity of existing solutions to their raw components
- Build up from those fundamentals, potentially creating something entirely new that fits the Korean context specifically

## SYNTHESIS: What This Means for a Tech Founder Starting TODAY in Korea

### Phase 0: Before Anything (Week 1-2)
1. **Identify a problem you personally have** (Paul Graham)
2. **Find your "secret"** — what truth do you know that others don't? (Thiel)
3. **Apply first principles** — break the problem down to fundamentals (Musk)
4. **Do NOT incorporate yet** — you don't need a legal entity to think, code, or talk to users

### Phase 1: Build (Week 2-8)
5. **Ship something embarrassing** (Reid Hoffman) — if it takes more than 60-90 days, you're overbuilding
6. **Use the modern solo-founder stack** — Cursor/Claude, Supabase, Vercel, Stripe
7. **Target a narrow market deeply** (Graham's "well" concept) — like Facebook starting at Harvard only
8. **Still do NOT incorporate** — you're still validating

### Phase 2: Get Users (Week 8-16)
9. **Do things that don't scale** (Graham) — manual user acquisition, personal onboarding
10. **Try the "Collison Installation"** — set up your product for users on the spot
11. **Talk to every user personally** — like Airbnb flying to NYC every week
12. **Make decisions with incomplete information** (Horowitz) — you'll never have enough data, act anyway

### Phase 3: Formalize (When Triggered)
13. **Incorporate when you must** — first payment, first hire, first investment, or co-founder formalization
14. **Choose co-founders you've known for years** (Thiel + Graham)
15. **Split equity near-equally** among co-founders (Seibel/YC)
16. **Keep CEO salary low** (Thiel) — equity alignment over cash comfort

### The Korean Advantage
- Korea's 2026 startup support budget is the largest in history (3.46 trillion won)
- Government programs now support non-face-to-face and AI-based businesses
- Evaluators prioritize execution (prototype + customers) over theory
- The fintech/delivery/e-commerce precedent (Toss, Coupang, Baemin) proves Korean founders can build world-class companies by starting scrappy and iterating relentlessly

### The Universal Truth

Every single success story above — Facebook, Apple, Stripe, Airbnb, Toss, Coupang, Baemin, Base44, HeadshotPro — started with **building something before formalizing anything**. The product came first. The users came second. The company structure came last.

> Paul Graham: "The one thing that's actually essential: making something people want."
